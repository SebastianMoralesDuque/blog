# When Tech Meets Illness: Power, Profit, and Cancer's Hidden Economy

The recent HackerNews surge of jjmaxwell4’s personal essay “Things to know when you get cancer” resonated far beyond its intimate narrative. It struck a chord because it names a truth many in tech-adjacent circles sense but rarely articulate: a cancer diagnosis is not merely a medical event; it is a gateway into a dense, profit-driven network of data extraction, software monopolies, and pharmaceutical gatekeeping. What follows is a critical walkthrough of the tech-industrial complex that activates the moment chronic illness enters the frame.

## The Electronic Health Record Oligopoly

![Imagen del artículo](https://image.pollinations.ai/prompt/A%20clean%20editorial%20photo%20of%20a%20hospital%20corridor%20with%20overhead%20fluorescent%20lights%2C%20linoleum%20floors%20receding%20into%20soft%20perspective%2C%20a%20single%20potted%20plant%20at%20the%20end%2C%20natural%20light%20from%20a%20side%20window%20casting%20subtle%20shadows%2C%20neutral%20gray%20and%20beige%20tones%2C%20realistic%20and%20grounded%20composition.?width=1200&height=675&model=flux&nologo=true&seed=25375)


Perhaps the most consequential bottleneck is the Electronic Health Record (EHR) system. In the United States, Epic Systems and (since its acquisition by Oracle in 2022) Cerner together hold over 50% of the hospital EHR market. Their platforms are not merely record-keepers; they are walled gardens. Patient histories, pathology reports, and treatment plans reside in proprietary formats that other providers struggle to read without costly middleware.



## Big Tech’s Health Ambitions

Beyond EHRs, the GAFAM cluster (Google, Apple, Facebook/Meta, Amazon, Microsoft) has aggressively expanded into health. Google’s DeepMind, originally celebrated for AI breakthroughs in protein folding, signed a controversial deal with the UK’s National Health Service to access millions of patient records for kidney disease research. Critics argued the consent process was opaque, and the data’s ultimate use remained tied to Google’s commercial AI roadmap.

Apple’s HealthKit and FDA-cleared ECG features on the Watch position the iPhone as a portable diagnostics hub. While empowering users with data access, Apple also controls the gate through which that data may be shared third-party. The company’s 2020 acquisition of Gliimpse, a health-data startup, signaled its intent to build a patient-owned data repository—one, however, still hosted on Apple’s infrastructure and subject to its terms.

Microsoft’s partnership with Nuance, an AI dictation company, integrates ambient clinical intelligence into exam rooms. The promise is reduced doctor burnout, but the underlying model captures verbatim patient-provider conversations, transcribing them into structured data that enriches Microsoft’s cloud and AI services.


## The Pharmaceutical-Medical Technology Complex

Take Illumina, the dominant genome-sequencing player. Its pricing power over sequencing data has drawn antitrust scrutiny in Europe and the U.S. When a cancer patient undergoes genomic profiling, the data often defaults to Illumina’s cloud, where it can be licensed to pharma partners. The concentration of sequencing capacity in a single entity creates a bottleneck analogous to the EHR duopoly: every diagnostic decision radiates through a corporate pipe.

Venture capital’s horizon—typically 7 to 10 years—means that many health startups are built for acquisition, not long-term patient benefit. The incentive structure optimizes for exit multiples, not treatment accessibility. This dynamic was succinctly captured in a 2022 Senate hearing on “drug pricing and innovation,” where executives admitted that a significant portion of R&D spending goes not to clinical trials but to patent protection and market segmentation strategies.

## Historical Roots and Structural Echoes


The transition from paper to digital records in the 1990s and 2000s was marketed as “efficiency,” but it also enabled a new kind of enclosure. Where once a physician’s notes were a private, portable memory aid, now they are structured, searchable, and monetizable datasets. The HITECH Act of 2009 allocated billions in subsidies for EHR adoption without imposing strong interoperability mandates. The result is the present-day oligopoly, where switching costs for hospitals run into the tens of millions.

## Toward Structural Remedies



Perhaps most importantly, the narrative must shift from “empowering the individual patient” to “democratizing the health data commons.” An individual with a cancer diagnosis already navigating a fragmented system is not equipped to negotiate data rights alone. Structural change—anti-monopoly enforcement, open standards funding, public investment in transparent research infrastructure—is the only leveler capable of confronting the power imbalances this essay exposes.
