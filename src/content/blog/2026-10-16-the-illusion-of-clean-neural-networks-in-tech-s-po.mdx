The recent proliferation of phrases like "neural networks are clean" across tech circles signals more than a technical milestone; it reflects a strategically polished narrative about the state of artificial intelligence. At first glance, the assertion that neural networks have reached a "clean" state suggests progress in interpretability, safety, or perhaps a reduction of unwanted behaviors. But framed within the broader economics of the AI industry, the terminology unveils a familiar pattern: the commodification of technical language to serve capital concentration and power realignment.

Goodfire, the company behind the referenced platform, epitomizes this dynamic. By positioning its tools as enabling "clean" neural network inspection, the startup taps into a lucrative discourse that venture capitalists and incumbents alike have been eager to finance. Goodfire’s recent funding rounds and partnerships with major cloud providers illustrate how "interpretability" has become a service layer in the AI stack, one that sits just above the opaque core models sold by the likes of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind. The "clean" branding functions as a differentiate: it promises regulators and users a semblance of control, while the underlying architecture remains concentrated in the hands of a few well-capitalized entities.



Historically, the free software movement of the 1990s and 2000s attempted to counter such concentration by championing open weights, transparent training processes, and community-driven audits. Figures like Eric S. Raymond and the early open-source AI advocates argued that "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow." Yet the current AI wave has seen many of those principles eclipsed by proprietary interests. Companies that once marketed open models have pivoted to closed-access APIs, citing safety and commercial viability. The result is a bifurcated landscape where "clean" interpretability is a premium feature, accessible primarily to well-funded corporations, while smaller actors rely on opaque, third-party services.


What does this mean for the trajectory of AI development? If the "clean" narrative continues to dominate without addressing the underlying power structures, we risk institutionalizing a two-tiered ecosystem: one where transparency is a marketable commodity, and another where the actual workings of neural networks remain insulated from scrutiny. The concentration of capital means that the direction of research, the definition of safety, and the interpretation of "clean" outcomes will be shaped by the same entities whose profit models depend on maintaining the status quo.

A more equitable path would require not just technical advances in model interpretability, but structural changes to how AI research and deployment are funded and governed. Open foundation models, democratized compute access, and independent audit mechanisms could disrupt the current concentration of power. Until then, the "clean" label risks serving as a polished façade over an economy defined by consolidation, venture-driven priorities, and the gradual erosion of collective oversight.

The conversation initiated by frameworks like Goodfire's is valuable, but it must move beyond buzzword compliance to critically examine who benefits from the "cleanliness" of neural networks, and who is left to interpret the fine print. Technological progress without corresponding distributions of power and information is, in economic terms, a transfer of value—from the many to the few—dressed in the language of clarity.
