# Sorry, I'm in a Meeting: Surveillance, Status, and the Architecture of Digital Occupancy


### From “Out to Lunch” to Asynchronous Surveillance



### The Platforms as Power Instruments


These platforms do not merely facilitate conversation; they construct the conditions under which “being in a meeting” is measured, stored, and commodified. Meeting duration, participant engagement (tracked through eye‑tracker patents and voice-activity detection), and even “breakout room” interactions become metadata that feeds into larger performance ecosystems. For a worker, the pressure to keep the green dot active, to keep the camera on, to participate in back‑channel chats—all of this is extracted from the simple act of showing up.

### Meetings as Extraction Engines

The economic logic of the modern meeting is straightforward: time is converted into data, and data is monetized. A one‑hour team sync on Zoom generates timestamps, vocal patterns, screen‑share activity, and facial recognition metrics. When aggregated across millions of users, this dataset becomes a product. Venture‑backed startups now sell “conversation intelligence” platforms that analyze meeting recordings for sentiment, speaker dominance, and “action item” completion rates. The data fuels predictive models about team productivity, turnover risk, and even “cultural fit.”


### Historical Echoes and Structural Repetition


The rhetoric has shifted from “management by walking around” to “management by status API,” but the underlying power relation remains. Who controls the status grid controls the perception of availability, and thus the distribution of tasks, promotions, and culpability. In this sense, the “sorry, I'm in a meeting” excuse is both a shield and a lever: it shields the individual from interruption while leveraging the collective assumption that meetings are productive, necessary, and beyond question.

### Resistance and the Rise of Async


Movements like the “Meeting-Free Wednesday” or the adoption of tools such as Notion, Fathom, and Fireflies—which record and summarize meetings without requiring live attendance—signal a desire to reclaim time. Yet these alternatives frequently land within the same platforms’ ecosystems, still generating metadata that can be mined. True resistance may require stepping outside the venture‑backed stack entirely: adopting open‑source video conferencing (Jitsi, BigBlueButton), insisting on written decision logs, or organizing around contractual limits on meeting time.

### Conclusion: What Are We Really Saying?

The next time you type or hear “Sorry, I'm in a meeting,”pause Consider what that phrase now occupies: not just a block on a calendar, but a data point in a surveillance architecture, a token of platform power, and a symptom of an economic system that expects every moment to be accounted for. The meeting excuse has become a ritual that both masks and reinforces the extraction of labor under the guise of collaboration. Recognizing this allows us to ask harder questions: whose time is being saved, whose is being monitored, and whether the meeting format we inherited is still fit for purpose—or merely a convenient alibi for the deepening concentration of capital in the digital age.
